4  Continuous Everywhere, Nowhere Differentiable

The Weierstrass function

Author

Nicholas Vantine

Published

Spring 2025

This chapter adapts the paper at source revision 612ce5f. It follows the paper’s historical motivation while tightening the main construction and proof.

4.1 Introduction

In the nineteenth century, continuity was often associated with geometric smoothness. Karl Weierstrass overturned that intuition by constructing a function that is continuous at every real number and differentiable at none (Wikipedia contributors 2025b, 2025a).

A classical form of his function is

\[ W(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a^n\cos(\pi b^n x), \]

where

\[ 0<a<1,\qquad b>1\text{ is an odd integer}, \qquad ab>1+\frac{3\pi}{2}. \]

The factor \(\pi\) is only a scaling convention: replacing \(x\) by \(x/\pi\) gives the version \(\sum a^n\cos(b^n x)\) used in the original paper. The series is built from smooth cosine waves whose amplitudes decay while their frequencies grow. The result converges to a continuous curve but retains oscillations at every scale (Stein and Shakarchi 2003).

4.2 Background

Definition 4.1 (Continuity) Let \(f:S\to\mathbb R\) and let \(c\in S\). The function \(f\) is continuous at \(c\) if, for every \(\varepsilon>0\), there exists \(\delta>0\) such that

\[ x\in S\text{ and }|x-c|<\delta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad |f(x)-f(c)|<\varepsilon. \]

This is the usual \(\varepsilon\)–\(\delta\) definition (Abbott 2015).

Definition 4.2 (Sequential continuity) Let \(f:S\to\mathbb R\) and let \(c\in S\). The function \(f\) is continuous at \(c\) if every sequence \((x_n)\) in \(S\) satisfying \(x_n\to c\) also satisfies \(f(x_n)\to f(c)\) (Anshelevich 2025).

Definition 4.3 (Differentiability) Let \(g:S\to\mathbb R\) be defined on an interval \(S\), and let \(c\in S\). The derivative of \(g\) at \(c\) is

\[ g'(c)=\lim_{x\to c}\frac{g(x)-g(c)}{x-c}, \]

provided this limit exists. In that case, \(g\) is differentiable at \(c\) (Abbott 2015).

Theorem 4.1 (Differentiability implies continuity) If \(g:S\to\mathbb R\) is differentiable at \(c\in S\), then \(g\) is continuous at \(c\).

Proof. Because the difference quotient tends to \(g'(c)\),

\[ \lim_{x\to c}\bigl(g(x)-g(c)\bigr) = \lim_{x\to c} \left(\frac{g(x)-g(c)}{x-c}\right)(x-c) =g'(c)\cdot0=0. \]

Therefore \(\lim_{x\to c}g(x)=g(c)\) (Abbott 2015).

The converse fails even for familiar functions.

Example 4.1 (The absolute-value cusp) The function \(f(x)=|x|\) is continuous at \(0\), but

\[ \lim_{x\to0^-}\frac{|x|}{x}=-1 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \lim_{x\to0^+}\frac{|x|}{x}=1. \]

Thus \(f'(0)\) does not exist (Abbott 2015).

This example has only one corner. Weierstrass’s construction is more striking because it has no differentiable point at all.

4.3 Construction

We need three elementary estimates.

Lemma 4.1 (Cosine is Lipschitz) For all \(x,y\in\mathbb R\),

\[ |\cos x-\cos y|\le |x-y|. \]

Proof. The mean value theorem gives \(\cos x-\cos y=-\sin(\xi)(x-y)\) for some \(\xi\) between \(x\) and \(y\). Since \(|\sin\xi|\le1\), the claim follows.

Lemma 4.2 (A nearby cosine oscillation) For every \(c\in\mathbb R\) and every positive integer \(k\), there exists

\[ y\in\left(c+\frac{\pi}{k},c+\frac{3\pi}{k}\right) \]

such that

\[ |\cos(kc)-\cos(ky)|\ge1. \]

Proof. As \(y\) traverses this interval, \(ky\) traverses an open interval of length \(2\pi\). Put \(r=\cos(kc)\). If \(-1<r<1\), the interval contains points where cosine equals both \(-1\) and \(1\), so one of those values differs from \(r\) by at least \(1\). If \(r=1\) or \(r=-1\), the interval contains a point where cosine is \(0\), again giving a difference of \(1\).

Lemma 4.3 (Integer shifts of cosine) For every integer \(r\) and every real \(t\),

\[ \cos(r\pi+t)=(-1)^r\cos t. \]

Proof. Apply the cosine addition formula and use \(\sin(r\pi)=0\) and \(\cos(r\pi)=(-1)^r\).

Lemma 4.4 (Three-term reverse triangle inequality) For all real numbers \(u,v,w\),

\[ |u+v+w|\ge |u|-|v|-|w|. \]

Proof. Twice applying the triangle inequality gives \(|u|\le |u+v+w|+|v|+|w|\).

Theorem 4.2 (Absolute convergence) For every \(x\in\mathbb R\), the series

\[ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a^n\cos(\pi b^n x) \]

converges absolutely.

Proof. Since \(|\cos(\pi b^n x)|\le1\),

\[ |a^n\cos(\pi b^n x)|\le a^n. \]

The geometric series \(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a^n\) converges because \(0<a<1\). Comparison proves absolute convergence (Massachusetts Institute of Technology 2020).

4.4 Continuity

Theorem 4.3 (Continuity of the Weierstrass function) The function

\[ W(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a^n\cos(\pi b^n x) \]

is bounded and continuous on \(\mathbb R\).

Proof. For every \(x\),

\[ |W(x)| \le \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a^n =\frac{1}{1-a}, \]

so \(W\) is bounded. The same estimate is independent of \(x\), so the Weierstrass M-test shows that the partial sums converge uniformly. Every partial sum is continuous, and a uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous (Massachusetts Institute of Technology 2020).

NoteSequential proof

The original paper also proves continuity using Definition 4.2. Let \(x_j\to c\) and fix \(\varepsilon>0\). Choose \(M\) so large that

\[ 2\sum_{n=M+1}^{\infty}a^n<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}. \]

We split the series into a finite head and a uniformly small tail. For the head, continuity of each cosine term gives

\[ \sum_{n=0}^{M}a^n \left|\cos(\pi b^n x_j)-\cos(\pi b^n c)\right| \longrightarrow0. \]

It is therefore less than \(\varepsilon/2\) for all sufficiently large \(j\). The tail satisfies

\[ \sum_{n=M+1}^{\infty}a^n \left|\cos(\pi b^n x_j)-\cos(\pi b^n c)\right| \le2\sum_{n=M+1}^{\infty}a^n <\frac{\varepsilon}{2}. \]

Hence \(|W(x_j)-W(c)|<\varepsilon\) eventually, so \(W(x_j)\to W(c)\) (Massachusetts Institute of Technology 2020).

The original paper approached continuity sequentially and used the following useful fact.

Exercise 4.1 (A limsup criterion for convergence to zero) Prove that

\[ \limsup_{n\to\infty}|x_n|=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \lim_{n\to\infty}x_n=0. \]

TipSolution

If \(x_n\to0\), then \(|x_n|\to0\), so \(\limsup |x_n|=0\).

Conversely, suppose \(\limsup |x_n|=0\). Because \(|x_n|\ge0\), we also have \(\liminf |x_n|\ge0\). Hence

\[ 0\le\liminf_{n\to\infty}|x_n| \le\limsup_{n\to\infty}|x_n|=0. \]

Thus \(|x_n|\to0\), which is equivalent to \(x_n\to0\).

4.5 Nowhere differentiability

Theorem 4.4 (Weierstrass) Assume

\[ 0<a<1, \qquad b>1\text{ is an odd integer}, \qquad ab>1+\frac{3\pi}{2}. \]

Then

\[ W(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a^n\cos(\pi b^n x) \]

is nowhere differentiable on \(\mathbb R\).

Proof. Fix \(x_0\in\mathbb R\). For each \(m\), choose an integer \(k_m\) nearest to \(b^m x_0\) and write

\[ z_m=b^m x_0-k_m, \qquad -\frac12\le z_m\le\frac12. \]

Set

\[ x_m=\frac{k_m+1}{b^m}, \qquad h_m=x_m-x_0=\frac{1-z_m}{b^m}. \]

Then

\[ \frac{1}{2b^m}\le h_m\le\frac{3}{2b^m}, \]

so \(x_m\to x_0\) from the right. Split the increment as

\[ W(x_m)-W(x_0)=A_m+B_m, \]

where \(A_m\) contains the terms \(0\le n<m\) and \(B_m\) contains the terms \(n\ge m\).

By Lemma 4.1,

\[ \begin{aligned} |A_m| &\le \pi h_m\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}(ab)^n \\ &\le \frac{\pi(ab)^m}{ab-1}h_m. \end{aligned} \]

For \(n\ge m\), the oddness of \(b\) and Lemma 4.3 give

\[ \cos(\pi b^n x_m)=-(-1)^{k_m} \]

and

\[ \cos(\pi b^n x_0) =(-1)^{k_m}\cos(\pi b^{n-m}z_m). \]

Therefore

\[ B_m=-(-1)^{k_m} \sum_{n=m}^{\infty}a^n \left(1+\cos(\pi b^{n-m}z_m)\right). \]

Every summand in parentheses is nonnegative. Keeping only the \(n=m\) term and using \(|z_m|\le1/2\) yields

\[ |B_m| \ge a^m\bigl(1+\cos(\pi z_m)\bigr) \ge a^m. \]

Since \(h_m\le 3/(2b^m)\),

\[ |B_m|\ge\frac23(ab)^m h_m. \]

The reverse triangle inequality now gives

\[ \left|\frac{W(x_m)-W(x_0)}{x_m-x_0}\right| \ge (ab)^m\left(\frac23-\frac{\pi}{ab-1}\right). \]

The parenthesized constant is positive precisely when \(ab>1+3\pi/2\), and \((ab)^m\to\infty\). Thus the difference quotients along \(x_m\to x_0\) are unbounded, so \(W'(x_0)\) does not exist. Because \(x_0\) was arbitrary, \(W\) is nowhere differentiable (Massachusetts Institute of Technology 2020).

4.6 Conclusion

The Weierstrass function separates continuity from smoothness in the strongest possible way. Its partial sums are smooth, and they converge uniformly, yet the limiting curve remains jagged at every scale. The example shows both the reach and the limits of geometric intuition: continuity survives uniform limits, while differentiability need not.
